When do you present?

Presentations are Thursday and Friday, 2 to 4 pm. There are six 20-minute slots each day, and each team takes one slot.

  • 12 minutes to talk.
  • 5 minutes for questions.
  • The last 3 minutes are for the next team to set up.

Sign up in the project presentations document. Write your title and names in an empty row.

The write-up has five parts

One write-up for everyone. A paper, an interactive article, and a blog post are all fine. Any format still has these five parts.

PartIn one lineWeight
Problem and motivationYour question or problem, what was known, and what you expected.15%
MethodYour setup: data, models, what you measured. Enough that a classmate could redo it.25%
Results and evaluationThe main figure with numbers, examples, and one comparison that rules out a boring explanation.25%
Limitations and failure casesFailures with your explanation. What it is meant for, what it is not for, and who it may work worse for.20%
ClarityReadable writing, labeled figures, and credit for everything you did not make.15%

Three ways to write it up

A short paper

Geirhos, Rubisch, Michaelis, Bethge, Wichmann and Brendel (2019). ImageNet-trained CNNs are biased towards texture. ICLR.

  • Problem. Do networks trained on ImageNet recognise objects by shape, as people do, or by texture?
  • Method. Cue-conflict images: the shape of one object with the texture of another, like a cat covered in elephant skin. People and networks label the same images.
  • Results. People mostly chose the shape. The networks mostly chose the texture.
  • A fix. Training on Stylized-ImageNet, where textures are swapped, made the network rely more on shape.
  • Limits. What can this experiment not tell us?

An interactive article

Olah, Mordvintsev and Schubert (2017). Feature Visualization. Distill.

  • Problem. What does a single neuron in a network respond to?
  • Method. Start from noise and change the image until the neuron fires as strongly as it can.
  • Results. Figures the reader explores: pick a neuron, see the images that drive it.
  • Failure cases. Left alone, the images fill with high-frequency noise. The article shows the fixes side by side.

A blog post

Karpathy (2014). What I learned from competing against a ConvNet on ImageNet.

  • Problem. How well does a person do on ImageNet, next to the best network?
  • Method. He built a labeling interface, trained himself, then labeled 1,500 test images.
  • Results. His error was 5.1%. The network’s was 6.8% on the same images.
  • Failure cases. The network missed small or thin objects. He struggled with dog breeds.

Before Thursday and Friday

  • Sign up for one slot in the sign-up document.
  • If you are showing a demo, record it.
  • Rehearse once with a timer.
  • Listen closely to the other talks. Assignment 2 is five questions on two of them.
  • Put your slides, write-up, and code in your project folder on Google Drive. What is there on the last day is what we grade.
  • Then tick the hand-in form: your project number and one checkbox.
