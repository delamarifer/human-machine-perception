Final project

Project
templates

One template for each step of the final project. Each step comes in two versions. Pick the one that fits your project.

Build a demo: make a working application of computer vision that someone can try. Run an experiment: explore a limitation of a vision model, or try to overcome one, and answer with a measurement.

The experiment track is a small rehearsal for your research project at the end of the year: a question, a written scientific report, and a presentation where you answer questions.

Step 1 · General idea

  • 2%
  • Due Saturday night
  • Google Form
  • Shared with the class
  • Our feedback by Saturday night

A few sentences, not a plan. We share every idea with the class so you can find teammates. Full credit for a complete, on-time answer.

Google Form questions
  1. Working title
  2. In one sentence, with no jargon: what do you want to make or find out?
  3. TrackBuild a demo / Run an experiment (explore or overcome a limitation) / Not sure yet
  4. Who would care, and why?A person who would use it, or a reason the answer matters.
  5. What images or video would you use, and where would they come from?Your phone, a public dataset, a website, or images from a science you care about (cells, crops, the sky). Name it if you know it.
  6. Is there a link to human vision?Optional. An illusion, a thing people do easily that models get wrong, or the other way round.
  7. Are you looking for teammates? What do you bring?For example: coding, design, a dataset, a domain you know.

Step 2 · Detailed proposal

  • 5%
  • Due Monday
  • 1–2 pages

Four sections, each worth a quarter of the grade. Answer every question in the template; short is fine. Upload your proposal.

Proposal Build a demo
  1. Problem and motivation 25% What will your demo do, in plain words? Who is it for, and what do they do today without it? What is hard about it for a computer?
  2. Approach 25% Input → steps → output: draw the pipeline as boxes. Which pretrained models will you use, and why those? What data will you test it on? What will someone see and do when they try it (a Colab notebook, a Gradio app, a phone photo)?
  3. Evaluation 25% How will you know it works? Name a test set you will collect (how many images, from where), what counts as a success on one example, and one number you will report. Which cases do you expect to fail?
  4. A realistic plan 25% The smallest version that works end to end, and the day you will have it. Then what you will add if there is time. The biggest risk, and your plan B if it happens. Who does what, if you are a team.
Proposal Run an experiment
  1. Problem and motivation 25% Your question, in one sentence. Say which kind it is: explore a limitation (where and why does a model fail?) or overcome one (does a change you make fix it?). What is already known (a paper, a demo, something we saw in class)? Why would the answer matter for how we build or trust vision models, or for what we know about human vision?
  2. Approach 25% Your prediction, written before you run anything, and the reason for it. What you will change (the conditions: for example, normal images vs. images with texture swapped). Which models you will compare. If you are trying to overcome a limitation: your fix (more data, augmentation, a different model, an idea from human vision) and the baseline you will compare it with. If people will judge images too, how (keep it anonymous, and talk to us first).
  3. Evaluation 25% What you will measure (accuracy, a similarity score, how often the model agrees with people). How many images per condition. Which result would support your prediction, and which would go against it.
  4. A realistic plan 25% The smallest version of the experiment (one model, two conditions) and the day you will have it. What you will add if there is time. The biggest risk, and your plan B. Who does what, if you are a team.

Step 3 · Meeting with a teacher

  • 3%
  • Between Monday and Wednesday
  • Book a time on Calendly

Full credit for coming prepared. The meeting is for getting unstuck, not for presenting.

Bring
  1. Your detailed proposal, open on your laptop.
  2. The one part you are least sure about.For example, whether a model can do it, or whether your data is enough.
  3. Three questions for us.
  4. Something running, if you have it: a pretrained model working on one of your images.
  5. Two or three example images from each condition, if you have them.

Final presentation

  • 15%
  • Last day
  • Time limit: announced in class

One slide per idea, in this order. The parts match the grading.

Slide outline Build a demo
  1. The idea 20%Who it is for and what it does, so the room gets it in the first minute. One picture of the problem.
  2. Demo 35%Show it working, live, on an example the audience has not seen. Have a screen recording ready in case the live demo fails.
  3. How it worksThe pipeline as boxes: input, models, output. One slide.
  4. ResultsYour one number on your test set, and two or three examples that work.
  5. Where it fails 20%Two or three failures, and your best guess at why.
  6. NextWhat you would build with another week.

Also graded: delivery, clear slides within time (15%), and answers to questions from the class (10%).

Slide outline Run an experiment
  1. The idea 20%Your question, and why the room should care, in the first minute. One example image.
  2. PredictionWhat you expected, and why, before you ran it.
  3. SetupThe conditions side by side, the models, and what you measured.
  4. Results 35%One figure with the main comparison. Say what it shows in one sentence. Then two or three examples.
  5. Limits 20%What your experiment cannot tell you: too few images, one model, a confound.
  6. What it meansFor how models see, and for how people see.

Also graded: delivery, clear slides within time (15%), and answers to questions from the class (10%). Treat the questions as a short rehearsal for the defence of your research project.

Final write-up

  • 20%
  • Last day
  • 3–4 pages + a link to the demo
  • 3–4 pages, like a short paper

Same five criteria for both tracks. What counts as evidence differs.

Write-up Build a demo
  1. Problem and motivation 15%Who it is for, what it does, and why it is hard for a computer.
  2. Method 25%A figure of the pipeline. The models and data, and why you chose them over the alternatives you tried. A link to your code and your demo, or a short video.
  3. Results and evaluation 25%Your test set (how many images, from where), your number, and a grid of examples that work. If you changed something along the way, a before-and-after.
  4. Limitations and failure cases 20%A grid of failures with your explanation of each. Then three short lists: what it is meant for, what it should not be used for, and who it might work worse for.
  5. Clarity 15%Readable writing, labeled figures, and credit for every model, dataset, and image you did not make.
Write-up Run an experiment
  1. Problem and motivation 15%The question, what was known before, and your prediction.
  2. Method 25%Your images and conditions, the models, what you measured, and how. Enough detail that a classmate could run it again.
  3. Results and evaluation 25%The main figure, with the numbers. Did the result match your prediction? If you tried a fix, how much better than the baseline, and on which cases? One control or comparison that rules out a boring explanation.
  4. Limitations and failure cases 20%What your experiment cannot show. Examples where the pattern breaks. What you would run next.
  5. Clarity 15%Readable writing, labeled figures, and credit for every model, dataset, image, and paper.

What these templates build on

We adapted these for a three-week course: shorter, plainer, and with a choice of track.

  • The Heilmeier Catechism (DARPA): the proposal questions. What are you trying to do, in plain words? Who cares? What are the risks? How will you check for success?
  • AsPredicted preregistration: the experiment proposal. Write the prediction, the conditions, the measure, and the sample size before running anything.
  • Model Cards for Model Reporting (Mitchell et al., 2019): the limitations section of the demo write-up. What it is meant for, what it is not, and who it may work worse for.
  • Stanford CS231n project guidelines: the sequence from proposal to final report and presentation.
  • CVPR author kit: an optional format for the experiment write-up, if you want it to look like a paper.
